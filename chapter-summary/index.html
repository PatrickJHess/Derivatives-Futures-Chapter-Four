📝 Chapter Summary

We considered three different genres of fixed-income futures:

  1. 3-Month SOFR futures that address the institutional borrowing market and are designed to facilitate interest rate swaps that link the needs of borrowers and lenders. These contracts have a deep calendar of liquidity. We used the Nelson model to estimate one- to five-year swap rates for 3-Month SOFR.

  2. 1-Month SOFR and 30 Day Fed Funds futures that are focused on short-term liquidity. Both SOFR and Fed funds rates live under the watchful eye of the Fed. These contracts call out breaks in the Fed’s action, allowing traders to bet on the efficacy of the Fed’s policies. Looking at the correlation of the contracts and how the spread trade varied, we demonstrated how the futures market anticipated chinks in the Fed’s armor.

  3. Treasury Bond and the related T-Note futures take an entirely different approach—they are settled with physical delivery as opposed to cash. This changes the way contracts are used and traded. We determined the CTD (Cheapest-to-Deliver) bond for the December 2026 Treasury Bond contract. That calculation demonstrated that:

    • Like index futures, the arbitrage band ($\Delta_t$) was positive. The no-arbitrage principle implies that the minimum value of $\Delta_t$ is about 40 BPS: in line with estimates made with S&P futures.

    • The pool of bonds that could be delivered into the contract is deep.
